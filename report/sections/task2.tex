\subsection{Corruption classifier}
\textbf{Architecture and training.} The classifier maps $\tilde{x}$ to $p=[p_{clean},p_{salt},p_{blur},p_{occ}]$. It has four stride-2 stages of two $3\times3$ convolutions (16, 32, 64, 128 channels, batch norm, ReLU, light Dropout2d), global average pooling, dropout and a linear layer; it has only 0.295\,M parameters. The predicted class is $r=\arg\max_k p_k$. Training minimises the multiclass cross-entropy, and every batch is \emph{exactly balanced}: a batch of size $B$ contains $B/4$ clean, salt-and-pepper, blur and occlusion items, with labels taken from the runtime corruption pipeline. Optuna tuned the learning rate, batch size, channel configuration, dropout and weight decay (objective: validation macro-F1); the selected values are learning rate $1.5\times10^{-3}$, batch 32, dropout 0.15 and weight decay $1.3\times10^{-4}$, trained for 30 epochs.

\textbf{Results.} On the full test protocol ($\nNEntries{}$ inputs) the classifier has accuracy \nClfAcc{} and macro-F1 \nClfMacroF{} (Table~\ref{tab:classifier}, Fig.~\ref{fig:cm}). Salt-and-pepper noise is recognised perfectly, because isolated black and white pixels are unmistakable. The \nMisrouteCount{} errors (\nMisroute\%) fall into two groups (Table~\ref{tab:misroute}): clean images taken for blurred or occluded ones, and low-severity occlusions taken for clean images. A 10\% occlusion can hide in a dark image, and a clean photograph with large uniform dark areas resembles a masked one. Because the classes are imbalanced in the full protocol (clean has a third of the support of each corruption), macro averages are reported next to the accuracy.

\subsection{Specialist autoencoders}
\textbf{Design.} Three specialists $A_{salt}$, $A_{blur}$, $A_{occ}$ share the architecture of the Task~1 autoencoder (channels 48--384, bottleneck 32, 4.01\,M parameters each) but have independently trained parameters, and each is trained only on its own corruption with the same clean target. Following the assignment's allowance for feasibility, one \emph{shared} Optuna search selects a common architecture: each trial trains all three specialists for six epochs on 1,000 images and scores the mean validation objective; learning rate, latent size, channels, batch size, dropout and $\alpha$ are tuned. The three specialists are then trained independently for 100 epochs on the full training set (learning rate $6.8\times10^{-4}$, batch 32, dropout 0.07, $\alpha=0.60$).

\textbf{Hard-routed inference.} The restored image is
\begin{equation}
\hat{x}=\begin{cases}
\tilde{x}, & r=\text{clean},\\
A_{salt}(\tilde{x}), & r=\text{salt},\\
A_{blur}(\tilde{x}), & r=\text{blur},\\
A_{occ}(\tilde{x}), & r=\text{occlusion},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
so clean inputs take an \emph{identity bypass} and are never processed by an expert. Two modes are evaluated: \emph{oracle} routing uses the true label from the manifest (it measures the specialists alone), \emph{predicted} routing uses the classifier (it measures the deployable system).

\subsection{Colour loss and its correction}\label{sec:colour}
Our first specialists (Optuna chose $\alpha=0.29$, i.e.\ 71\% of the loss on $1-\mathrm{SSIM}$) produced restorations that were visibly washed-out, sepia-toned versions of the input. Measuring colourfulness (mean of the per-pixel maximum minus minimum RGB channel) on \nColourN{} salt-and-pepper test inputs confirmed it: the first salt-and-pepper specialist kept only \nColSpSpecFirst\% of the clean image's colour and the first soft mixture \nColSpMoeFirst\%, whereas the universal autoencoder kept \nColSpUniversal\% (Table~\ref{tab:colour}). SSIM compares local structure and is weakly sensitive to a global chroma shift, so a loss dominated by the SSIM term barely penalises discarding colour; the Optuna objective ($0.5\,\mathcal{L}_1+0.5(1-\mathrm{SSIM})$) did not catch it either. We restricted the $\alpha$ search to $[0.55,0.95]$ and repeated the specialist and mixture training: the final specialist ($\alpha=0.60$) keeps \nColSpSpecFinal\% and the final mixture \nColSpMoeFinal\%. The effect is weaker on occlusion inputs (first specialist \nColOcSpecFirst\%, final specialist \nColOcSpecFinal\%, universal \nColOcUniversal\%, mixtures \nColOcMoeFinal\%), and the final occlusion specialist still keeps slightly less colour than the universal model. Table~\ref{tab:v1v2} shows the side effect on the error measures: the L1 error of the deployed hard-routed system falls by about \nLOneDropPct\% (\nHardPredLOneFirst{} to \nHardPredLOneFinal{}) while SSIM is essentially unchanged, as expected when the loss again weights pixel accuracy. Both runs are kept in the repository (tags without and with \code{\_v2}).

\subsection{Routing results}
Table~\ref{tab:routing} compares the corrupted input, the universal autoencoder, oracle routing and predicted routing for each input type. Predicted routing (overall SSIM \nHardPredSSIM{}) is indistinguishable from oracle routing (\nHardOracleSSIM{}) because the classifier is accurate. Its advantage over the universal model (\nTOneSSIMall{}) is small and comes entirely from clean images (SSIM \nHardSSIMclean{} against \nTOneSSIMclean{}, which also raises the PSNR of the clean class far above the 26\,dB of the universal model). On the corrupted types the specialists are \emph{slightly worse} than the universal model in SSIM: \nHardSSIMsaltpepper{} against \nTOneSSIMsaltpepper{} for noise, \nHardSSIMblur{} against \nTOneSSIMblur{} for blur and \nHardSSIMocclusion{} against \nTOneSSIMocclusion{} for occlusion (PSNR: \nHardPSNRsaltpepper{}, \nHardPSNRblur{} and \nHardPSNRocclusion{}\,dB against \nTOnePSNRsaltpepper{}, \nTOnePSNRblur{} and \nTOnePSNRocclusion{}\,dB). A single specialist has the same capacity and the same bottleneck as the universal model, so specialisation brings no extra reconstruction ability; its hyper-parameters were chosen by one shared search for three models, and its batch size (32) gives half as many updates per epoch as the universal model's (16), which may explain part of the small gap. The measured benefit of routing is therefore the identity bypass.

\textbf{Classifier-caused failures.} The mis-routings have opposite consequences (Table~\ref{tab:misroute}). The \nMisClean{} clean images sent to a blur or occlusion expert are real failures: SSIM changes by \nMisCleanLoss{} on average relative to the oracle route ($-0.13$ for those sent to the blur expert, $-0.25$ for those sent to the occlusion expert). In Fig.~\ref{fig:mis} a clean photograph of a dog on grass is taken for blurred and ``restored'' into a brown, smeared scene (SSIM 0.51 instead of 1.00), a photograph with black letterbox bars and a cat on a black background are taken for occluded images and the occlusion expert invents content for the dark regions (0.54, 0.57), and a colourful image is taken for blurred (0.60). The 128 occlusions labelled ``clean'' (126 of them 10\% masks) are left untouched, which scores about $+0.12$ SSIM \emph{higher} than the oracle route, but only because SSIM ignores the untouched black rectangle (Section~\ref{sec:t1}); the hole remains visible, so these are failures of the system even though the metric rewards them. Averaged over all inputs, mis-routing changes the mean SSIM by only $+0.0001$, because the two groups nearly cancel.

\textbf{Other failures.} Figure~\ref{fig:fail2} shows the four lowest-SSIM outputs of the system: as for the universal model they are 35\% occlusions that hide much of the animal (a beagle on grass, a basset hound, a dark terrier, a brown dog among flowers; SSIM 0.28--0.33). They are not routing errors. The specialist fills the hole with a smooth, brownish patch of texture even where the surroundings are green, a mild local colour error consistent with the lower colour retention of the occlusion specialist (Table~\ref{tab:colour}). Training curves of the classifier and of the specialists are in Fig.~\ref{fig:curves2}.
